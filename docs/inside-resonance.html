Chapter 1

What this thing actually is

A music player with no music, no server and no account — assembled out of three other people's APIs.

Resonance owns nothing. It does not host audio, does not have a catalogue, and does not know what a song sounds like. What it owns is a taste profile and the judgement about what to play next — and everything else is borrowed.

the only thing that is yours

The taste profile

Loved, liked, disliked, banned, genre scores, artist scores — in localStorage. Exportable and importable. Lose it and the app is a stranger again.

the brain

Gemini

Given the profile as a prompt, it returns six songs as JSON. It is the recommendation engine, and the prompt is the entire algorithm.

the catalogue

YouTube

Turns “Title — Artist” into a playable video id, and the IFrame player actually plays it. The only viable free, embeddable, in-browser audio source.

the ground truth

Last.fm

Optional. Supplies a candidate pool of what real listeners actually play alongside a track — so Gemini curates rather than recalls.

The sentence that explains the whole design

Resonance keeps the taste and rents everything else. That is why there is no backend, no account, no catalogue licence and no hosting cost — and it is also why the app is fragile in a very specific way: three separate quotas can run out, and Chapter 5 is entirely about surviving that.

Chapter 2

Four signals, and the cases where they must not fire

The learning model is thirty lines. Most of them are exceptions, and the exceptions are the design.

SignalMeaningWeight
Finish a songYou like it+1 genre, +1 artist
SkipNot your type−1 genre, −1 artist
♥ HeartAll-time favourite; keeps playing+3 genre, +3 artist
⦸ Never againThis exact song, banned−2 genre — a nudge, not a ban
next — teaches nothingA deliberate no-signal escapenothing

That last row matters more than it looks. Without a neutral skip, every “I am not in the mood for this right now” is recorded as “I dislike this”, and the profile slowly learns a version of you that only exists on bad days. Giving the user a way to say nothing is what keeps the signal clean.

Read the guards

Three of these functions begin by refusing to learn. Finishing a hearted song does not demote it to merely “liked”. Skipping a favourite is “still a favourite, just moving on”. And nothing at all is learned in favourites mode, because in that mode the user is not making a judgement — they already made it.

A feedback system that records every interaction as a preference will corrupt itself. Knowing which interactions are not preferences is the whole skill.

Chapter 3

The prompt is the algorithm

Eighty lines that assemble a request. This is where the product actually lives.

In most apps the prompt is a string with a few variables in it. Here it is a composed document — built from an array of conditional lines, filtered and joined — where each line is a rule that applies only in certain modes, and the modes have a defined precedence.

Precedence, made explicit

Look at the comments on lines 893 and 899. “similarity pool contradicts exploration” — in exploration mode the Last.fm pool is skipped entirely, because a pool of similar tracks is the opposite of what exploration means. And “a station outranks the radio lane” — two mutually exclusive ways of choosing the next song, with the tie broken in one place.

Modes that quietly combine produce incoherent output and are almost impossible to debug, because there is no error — just recommendations that feel wrong. Declaring precedence in code, with the reason in a comment, is how you stop that.

Five techniques worth naming

  • Sampling, not truncating. Line 913 uses sampleDiverse across the full history rather than the last twenty. Taking the most recent twenty would make the profile amnesiac about everything you liked last month.
  • An explicit avoid-list. Line 919 sends the last 45 keys played or queued. Cheaper and more reliable than hoping the model does not repeat itself.
  • Grounding beats recall. With a Last.fm key, the candidate pool comes from what real people actually play alongside the seed, and Gemini only curates it. The model is much better at judging a list than at remembering one.
  • Constraints as counts. “Never more than 2 songs of the same genre in one batch”, “at most one exploration pick”. Numbers are followed; adjectives are interpreted.
  • The output shape is dictated. Line 933 gives the exact JSON, field by field, including "youtubeId": "11-char id if you are confident, else empty string" — which invites the model to volunteer a shortcut without punishing it for not knowing.

Chapter 4

Always two songs ahead

The pipeline that makes an AI player feel instant, when every step of it is slow.

Asking Gemini for a song takes seconds. Resolving it to a YouTube id takes another request. If either happened when the current song ended, every track would be followed by a silence — which is the death of a music player.

So there are two buffers, filled at different rates.

The two-stage buffer

Candidates are Gemini's suggestions — title, artist, genre, reason. Cheap to hold, not yet playable. Refilled six at a time whenever fewer than three remain.

Queue is resolved, playable tracks with a video id. Kept at two, because two is enough to cover the gap between songs and small enough that a change of taste is not stuck behind a long queue.

One expensive call produces six cheap items; each cheap item costs one smaller call to become playable. Batching the expensive step and streaming the cheap one is the general shape of every responsive AI feature.

Two details worth pulling out. guard++ < 14 on line 1051 bounds the loop — every candidate can legitimately be rejected (banned genre, already seen, unresolvable), and without a bound an unlucky batch spins forever. And line 1030's artist lane bypasses Gemini entirely: when the pool is one discography there is nothing to recommend, so the batch is free.

Chapter 5

Living on free tiers

Three APIs, three quotas, and a surprising amount of the codebase devoted to what happens when they run dry.

This is the tax on the Chapter 1 bargain. Owning nothing means depending on three services that will, eventually and at the worst moment, say no.

Four failures, four different responses

404 — the model id was retired. Switch to gemini-flash-latest and retry. This is why dated model ids are a time bomb and rolling aliases are not.

429 — this key is spent. Rotate to the next key silently and retry; only when every key is exhausted does it become an error.

503 — overloaded. Wait 2.5 seconds and retry exactly once. Retrying forever turns a transient wobble into a hang.

400/403 — inspect the message. A rejected or leaked key rotates like a spent one; anything else falls through as a real error, because papering over a genuine bug is worse than showing it.

Multiple keys per provider is the interesting structural choice. Free tiers are per-key, so a user with three Gemini keys has three times the quota — and the rotation is invisible except for a small toast. quotaRecovered() then reopens the door after a cooldown, because a daily quota is not a permanent one.

The most important line, though, is the classification itself: a 429 is transient and a 400 is not. Every project in your folder that talks to a model has rediscovered this, and it is worth stating once as a rule: retry on rate limits and server errors, fail loudly on anything that means you made a mistake.

Chapter 6

The car, and the button that means the opposite

A Capacitor wrapper for one capability a browser cannot have, and one design decision that is smarter than it first looks.

On Android, a browser PWA cannot own a real media session. No steering-wheel buttons, no lock-screen controls, no reliable background audio. Those are the entire reason car/ exists — it ships the same web app inside a native shell that can register one.

The wheel mapping

⏮ Previous → ♥ Favourite (keeps playing, learns at 3× weight). ⏭ Next → skip onward, and deliberately learns nothing.

This looks wrong until you think about the context. You are driving. There are two buttons under your thumb and you cannot look at them. What do you actually want to do to a song at 100 km/h?

You want to say “this one — remember this”, and you want to say “not this, move on”. You almost never want to hear the previous song again, because on a station that never repeats there is no previous song worth returning to.

So the two most reachable controls are mapped to the two things a driver actually needs, and the label on the physical button is simply wrong. That is the right call — fit the control to the situation, not to the icon printed on it.

Note also that Next teaches nothing in the car. In the browser a skip is a negative signal, because you skipped deliberately while looking at the screen. In a car a skip might be a passenger, a wrong turn of the wheel, or a song that is fine but not right for this road. The same gesture carries less information, so it is recorded as less.

The APK is built by GitHub Actions — no local Android SDK — and Capacitor 6 is pinned on purpose. That pin is a decision, not neglect: the media session plugin here is hand-written Java against a specific Capacitor plugin API, and a major upgrade means rewriting it.

Check yourself: why does the car's Next button teach nothing when the browser's skip teaches a dislike?
Chapter 7

Change it yourself

Four exercises, in order of difficulty. Each one uses a different layer of what you have just read.

  1. Change a weight. A heart is +3, a like is +1. Make hearts +5 and listen for an afternoon. This is the fastest way to feel what a weight in a recommender actually controls — and to notice that it takes a long time to feel anything, which is itself the lesson about tuning recommenders.
  2. Add a prompt rule. One line in the lines array — “never two songs by the same artist within four picks”, say. Then check that it does not contradict an existing rule, which is the actual skill: a prompt is a document with internal consistency, not a bag of instructions.
  3. Add a radio lane. SEGMENTS is data. A new lane is an object with a laneDesc, and the prompt machinery already handles precedence against stations and exploration.
  4. Fix the open bugs. Two are on the board: artist merge, and songs reporting wrong titles and artist names. Both live in the resolve step, where a model's guess at a title meets YouTube's actual metadata — which is the messiest seam in the app and therefore the most instructive place to work.

Running it

There is nothing to build. Open cassette/index.html from a local server (the service worker needs an origin) and enter a Gemini key. A YouTube Data API key is recommended for reliable search; Last.fm is optional and noticeably improves station quality.

The APK builds in CI only: Actions → Cassette car APK → Run workflow. No local Android SDK is needed, and none is expected to work.

Habits that separate professionals from hobbyists

  • Know which interactions are not preferences. Half the feedback code is guards, and they are why the profile stays clean.
  • Give the user a way to say nothing. A neutral skip prevents a profile that only knows your bad days.
  • Ground the model rather than trusting its memory. Real listening data as a candidate pool, with the model curating, beats asking it to recall.
  • Classify failures before handling them. 404, 429, 503 and 400 mean four different things and deserve four different responses.
  • Fit the control to the situation. PREVIOUS means favourite because that is what a driver needs, whatever the button says.
  • When you cannot explain it, you do not understand it yet. Try explaining why finishing a hearted song must not add it to “liked”. The gaps announce themselves.
